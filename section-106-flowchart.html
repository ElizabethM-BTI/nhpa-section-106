Overview

Section 106 in Brief

Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act requires every federal agency to look at how a proposed project might affect historic places before the agency approves the project. This flowchart walks through the six steps of that review — from the moment the agency proposes an action all the way through negotiating protections for historic properties or, if negotiations fail, escalating to the head of the agency for a final decision. Along the way, the map shows how three agencies — FERC, the Army Corps of Engineers, and BLM — change the standard process when the project is a pipeline, transmission line, or other linear energy infrastructure.

§800.3

Initiation

The federal agency (such as the Bureau of Land Management, the Army Corps of Engineers, or the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission) figures out whether Section 106 applies to the proposed project and starts building the team that will carry out the review. The agency official must determine whether the project is an "undertaking" under the law, identify which State or Tribal Historic Preservation Officers have jurisdiction, plan how to involve the public, and invite tribes, local governments, and other groups to join the consultation — all before the agency approves the project.

If the state or tribal preservation office doesn't respond to the agency's request for review within 30 days, the agency can move forward to the next step or consult with the Council instead — per §800.3(c)(4). In practice, many agency staff are hesitant to proceed without a response, especially from a THPO, even though the regulations allow it.

FERC Pre-filing or Direct Track
Interstate natural gas pipelines may use FERC's pre-filing process (18 C.F.R. §157.21) to begin §106 consultation up to three years before the formal application. Outside pre-filing, FERC staff initiate consultation upon receipt of the application with a formal letter to SHPO/THPO.
USACE PCN-Triggered Initiation
For NWP 12 pipeline water crossings, §106 is triggered only when the applicant submits a Pre-Construction Notification (PCN). The district verifies the APE and notifies SHPOs/THPOs. No PCN means no NWP 12 §106 review.
BLM Annual Work Plan
BLM linear projects crossing federal land are initiated through the Annual Work Plan (AWP) process. The ROW grant application triggers §106, requiring BLM to define the full APE for the federal land segment — even when construction will proceed in phases.

§800.4

Identification of Historic Properties

The agency official must make a genuine effort to find every historic property inside the Area of Potential Effects — including archaeological sites, historic buildings, districts, landscapes, and places of cultural significance to tribes. What counts as a "genuine effort" depends on the size and nature of the project. Each agency has its own rules about how much ground to survey and what methods to use, so the scope of fieldwork looks very different at FERC, the Army Corps, and BLM.

FERC Historic & Cultural Resources Report
Applicants must produce an HCRR covering all resources within the APE. For interstate pipelines, the APE typically extends 0.5 miles on each side of the right-of-way. Phase I archaeological surveys are required for all ground-disturbing activities; Phase II/III surveys follow where potentially eligible resources are found.
USACE Self-Certification (GC 20)
NWP 12 applicants self-certify compliance with General Condition 20. The applicant conducts its own cultural resources survey and submits a report to SHPO/THPO for concurrence. Self-certification is not available in areas of high archaeological sensitivity.
BLM GIS Risk Model (100% / 50% / 10%)
BLM uses a GIS-based archaeological probability model to assign survey intensity: 100% coverage, 50% transect, or 10% visual inspection. Areas within 500 ft of water are automatically classified as high-sensitivity (100% survey required).

§800.4(d)

Results of Identification

After the surveys are done, the agency official and the SHPO or THPO evaluate each property the agency found and decide whether that property qualifies for the National Register of Historic Places. If the agency and the preservation officer agree that no eligible properties exist inside the Area of Potential Effects, the agency notifies all consulting parties and the Section 106 review is finished.

FERC Evaluation Report
The applicant submits an Evaluation Report to FERC with National Register eligibility determinations agreed on by SHPO/THPO. If SHPO/THPO disagrees, the matter is referred to ACHP for guidance. FERC then issues a determination letter accepting the finding or requesting additional information.
USACE GC 20 Verification
The USACE district engineer verifies that surveys were conducted, SHPO/THPO concurred on eligibility, and no historic properties are affected (or conditions are imposed). For complex projects, USACE may require an Individual Permit rather than NWP 12.
BLM Interim Report (30-Day Review)
The applicant submits an Interim Report within 30 days of completing field surveys, listing all identified properties, eligibility recommendations, and a site map. BLM's archaeologist has 30 days to concur or request additional information.
§800.5

Assessment of Adverse Effects

The agency official applies the adverse effect test to determine whether the project could change the qualities that make a historic property significant — including physical damage, visual intrusions, noise, or changes to the property's setting. The agency makes this determination with input from the SHPO or THPO, tribes, and other consulting parties. This finding is the most consequential decision in the entire Section 106 process.

FERC 25 ft² Ground Disturbance Rule
FERC established that ground disturbance greater than 25 ft² within the APE triggers an adverse effect finding if eligible properties are within the project footprint. A Finding of No Historic Properties Affected (FNHPA) is issued if no eligible properties fall within the APE.
USACE GC 20 Bars NWP on Adverse Effect
An adverse effect finding prevents use of NWP 12. The applicant must modify the project to avoid the effect and re-apply, seek an Individual Permit, or negotiate an MOA with SHPO/THPO. Adverse effect findings are therefore highly consequential for NWP 12 users.
BLM Annual Fieldwork Report (90 Days)
BLM requires an Annual Fieldwork Report within 90 days of completing fieldwork. Adverse effect identification triggers SHPO notification within 15 days and a mandatory 30-day public comment period before formal §800.5 consultation begins.

§800.6

Resolution of Adverse Effects

When the agency finds that the project will adversely affect historic properties, the agency official must negotiate with the SHPO or THPO, the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation, tribes, applicants, and other consulting parties to find ways to avoid, reduce, or offset those adverse effects. The parties record their agreement in a legally binding document — either a Memorandum of Agreement for a single project or a Programmatic Agreement for complex or multi-project situations.

FERC FERC Staff Drafts MOA/PA
FERC staff attorneys draft the MOA or PA, not the applicant. The applicant is a consulting party; FERC is the lead federal agency signatory. For large pipeline projects spanning multiple states, FERC typically uses a Programmatic Agreement (PA) covering multiple SHPOs/THPOs.
USACE GC 20 Permit Conditions
For NWP 12, resolution occurs through permit conditions rather than a formal MOA. Conditions may include archaeological monitoring, pre-construction testing, inadvertent discovery plans, and reporting requirements. Failure to comply can result in suspension of the NWP authorization.
BLM PA Before Construction; Treatment Plans 120 Days
BLM requires PA execution before any ground-disturbing activity. Treatment Plans for identified historic properties must be approved within 120 days of PA execution. The BLM State Director signs the PA, which must include discovery procedures, monitoring protocols, and reporting requirements.

§800.7

Failure to Resolve Adverse Effects

If the parties cannot agree on how to resolve the adverse effects on historic properties, any party can terminate the consultation. The Advisory Council on Historic Preservation then prepares formal written comments and sends those comments to the head of the entire agency. The head of the agency — not the project manager — must personally review the Council's comments and make the final decision. The law says this responsibility cannot be handed off to anyone else.

FERC Full Commission Decision
§800.7 matters are referred to the full FERC Commission (all five commissioners), not staff. The full Commission must consider ACHP's formal comments before approving or rejecting the project. This is a rare procedural posture — most FERC §106 disputes resolve before this stage.
USACE District Commander Decision
For NWP 12, the District Commander (not the project manager or district engineer) makes the final §800.7 decision and must provide written documentation of how ACHP's comments were considered. District Commander decisions can be challenged in federal court under the APA.
BLM Escalation to Secretary of Interior
§800.7 failure is rare for BLM projects. The BLM State Director first evaluates whether modification or enhanced mitigation is possible. If not, the matter escalates to the Secretary of the Interior, whose decision is subject to judicial review under the APA.

Why these three projects: Gateway West, SunZia, and the Atlantic Coast Pipeline are the largest recent linear energy projects where Section 106 became a significant source of delay, cost overruns, or legal challenge. Each one illustrates a different failure mode — intergovernmental coordination, deferred identification, and tribal consultation adequacy — that the standard §106 process was designed to prevent.
Route: ~1,000 miles, 10 segments, Glenrock WY to Melba ID
Proponents: Idaho Power & PacifiCorp
Lead agency: BLM
Status: Segments 1-7, 10 built
Timeline: PA signed Sept 2013, amended Feb 2016; Segments 8-9 required separate EA (Jan 2018) and congressional attention (H.R. 2104)

Gateway West is a 1,000-mile transmission line that Idaho Power and PacifiCorp proposed to carry electricity from Wyoming wind farms to population centers in Idaho. BLM was the lead federal agency. The project had to cross two states, 10 segments, and the jurisdictions of multiple SHPOs and THPOs. The §106 review was not driven by a lawsuit — it was driven by the sheer difficulty of coordinating historic property reviews across that many jurisdictions at the same time. BLM signed a Programmatic Agreement in 2013 and then had to amend it twice because the original process could not keep up with the scale of the project. Two of the 10 segments (8 and 9) got stuck for years because of routing disputes near the Morley Nelson Snake River Birds of Prey National Conservation Area, requiring a separate Environmental Assessment and a bill introduced in Congress by Representative Mike Simpson.

Where the Process Broke Down
I1 — Identify SHPO/THPO Agency Action §800.3(c)
Should have happened
BLM identifies all SHPOs and THPOs with jurisdiction at the outset and initiates consultation with each.
Actually happened
The 1,000-mile route crossed Wyoming and Idaho, each with its own SHPO and different eligibility standards. Coordinating between two state offices with different timelines, priorities, and evaluation criteria delayed the entire review.
Consequence
The PA had to be amended twice to reconcile the two states' approaches, adding years to the timeline.
I3 — Identify Consulting Parties Agency Action §800.3(f)
Should have happened
BLM identifies all consulting parties — tribes, local governments, organizations — and invites them to participate before the review begins.
Actually happened
Across 1,000 miles and 10 segments, the number of consulting parties was enormous. Coordinating input from tribes, counties, conservation groups, and ranchers along the entire corridor overwhelmed the standard consultation process.
Consequence
Segments 8 and 9 became bottlenecked by local opposition and routing disputes near the Birds of Prey NCA that the initial consulting-party identification had not adequately anticipated.
ID1 — Define APE Agency Action §800.4(a)(1)
Should have happened
BLM defines the Area of Potential Effects for the entire project before beginning identification.
Actually happened
A single APE for 1,000 miles of diverse terrain — from Wyoming high plains to Idaho river canyons — was impractical. BLM used a phased approach, defining APEs segment by segment, but segments were at different stages simultaneously.
Consequence
The phased APE approach, while authorized by §800.4(b)(2), meant that later segments discovered issues that earlier segments had already passed through, requiring rework.
ID3 — Phased Identification SHPO Review §800.4(b)(2)
Should have happened
Phased identification is authorized for corridors, but the agency must establish the likely presence of historic properties for each segment through background research and consultation.
Actually happened
The phased approach worked for segments 1–7 and 10 but broke down for segments 8 and 9, where the route passed through archaeologically and ecologically sensitive areas near the Snake River that required a fundamentally different level of survey effort.
Consequence
Segments 8 and 9 required a separate EA (January 2018) and congressional intervention, delaying those segments by years beyond the rest of the project.
Q_ELIG — Eligibility Determination Decision Point §800.4(c)(2)
Should have happened
The agency and SHPO/THPO agree on National Register eligibility for each identified property using the same criteria.
Actually happened
Wyoming and Idaho SHPOs applied eligibility criteria differently — the same type of resource could be evaluated as eligible in one state and not in the other, creating inconsistencies along the route.
Consequence
BLM had to negotiate separate protocols with each SHPO through PA amendments to harmonize eligibility standards across the two-state route.
Key Takeaway: Gateway West shows that the §106 process was designed for individual projects in a single jurisdiction. When a linear project crosses 1,000 miles, two states, and 10 segments, the standard process — even with a Programmatic Agreement — struggles to coordinate the number of reviewing agencies, consulting parties, and eligibility decisions that all have to happen in parallel. The project was not stopped by litigation; it was slowed by the structural mismatch between the scale of linear infrastructure and the case-by-case design of §106.
Route: ~550 miles, central NM to southern AZ, two 500-kV lines
Proponents: Southwestern Power Group (2006), sold to Pattern Energy (2022)
Lead agency: BLM
Status: Active litigation
Timeline: Proposed 2006; BLM ROD Jan 2015; construction authorized Sept 2023; Ninth Circuit remand 2024

SunZia is a 550-mile transmission line proposed in 2006 to carry wind-generated electricity from central New Mexico to Arizona. BLM approved the route in January 2015, running it through the San Pedro Valley in southeastern Arizona. BLM used a Programmatic Agreement to defer the identification of historic properties until after the approval decision — meaning the agency said yes to the route before it knew what historic places were in the way. BLM did not recognize the San Pedro Valley as a potential Traditional Cultural Property until 2023, and did not begin government-to-government consultation with the Tohono O'odham Nation and San Carlos Apache Tribe until 2023 — more than a decade after the project was proposed. BLM authorized construction in September 2023 before completing identification. The tribes and Archaeology Southwest filed suit in January 2024. The district court dismissed the case, but the Ninth Circuit reversed and remanded, finding that the plaintiffs plausibly alleged NHPA violations.

Where the Process Broke Down
I3 — Identify Consulting Parties Agency Action §800.3(f)(2)
Should have happened
BLM makes a reasonable and good faith effort to identify Indian tribes that might attach religious and cultural significance to historic properties in the APE, and invites them to be consulting parties.
Actually happened
Government-to-government consultation with the Tohono O'odham Nation and San Carlos Apache Tribe did not begin until 2023 — over a decade after the project was proposed and eight years after BLM issued the ROD.
Consequence
By the time tribes were brought into the process, the route had already been approved and construction authorized. The tribes had no meaningful opportunity to shape the outcome before the decision was made.
ID3 — Phased Identification SHPO Review §800.4(b)(2)
Should have happened
Phased identification is allowed for large corridors, but the agency must establish the likely presence of historic properties through background research and consultation before making the approval decision.
Actually happened
BLM used the Programmatic Agreement to defer identification until after the 2015 ROD. The agency approved the route without completing — or in some areas, beginning — the identification of historic properties along the corridor.
Consequence
BLM authorized construction (Limited Notices to Proceed) in September 2023 in areas where identification was still incomplete, directly contradicting the requirement that identification occur before the agency approves the undertaking.
Q4 — No Historic Properties Finding Decision Point §800.4(d)(1)
Should have happened
The agency finds that no historic properties are present or that the undertaking will have no effect, documents this finding, and provides it to the SHPO/THPO and consulting parties for review.
Actually happened
BLM effectively proceeded as though no historic properties were affected in areas where identification had not been completed. The San Pedro Valley — which the Tohono O'odham Nation and San Carlos Apache Tribe consider culturally significant — was not evaluated as a potential Traditional Cultural Property until 2023.
Consequence
The Ninth Circuit found that plaintiffs plausibly alleged NHPA violations, reversing the district court's dismissal and remanding the case for further proceedings.
Q_ELIG — Eligibility Determination Decision Point §800.4(c)(1)
Should have happened
The agency, guided by the Secretary's Standards, applies the National Register criteria to identified properties — recognizing that tribes possess special expertise in assessing eligibility of properties with religious and cultural significance.
Actually happened
BLM did not evaluate the San Pedro Valley as a potential Traditional Cultural Property until 2023, despite the valley's documented significance to multiple tribes. The failure to recognize a potential TCP meant the eligibility question was never asked during the original review.
Consequence
Construction proceeded in an area where a potentially eligible historic property had not been identified, let alone evaluated — the exact scenario §106 was designed to prevent.
Key Takeaway: SunZia illustrates the danger of deferring identification past the decision point. The Programmatic Agreement mechanism — designed to let agencies phase their work on large corridors — was used to postpone identification until after the ROD, after construction authorization, and after ground was broken. When the agency does not know what historic properties exist before it says yes, the "take into account" requirement of §106 becomes meaningless. The Ninth Circuit's decision to revive the case signals that courts will scrutinize post-hoc §106 compliance for linear projects.
Route: 600 miles, WV through VA to NC, 42-inch natural gas
Proponents: Dominion Energy & Duke Energy
Lead agency: FERC
Status: Cancelled July 2020
Timeline: Proposed 2014; FERC certificate Oct 2017; cancelled July 2020; final cost ~$8 billion (original estimate $5.1 billion)

The Atlantic Coast Pipeline was a 600-mile, 42-inch natural gas pipeline proposed by Dominion Energy and Duke Energy to run from West Virginia through Virginia to North Carolina. FERC issued a certificate in October 2017. The project immediately ran into legal challenges — the Fourth Circuit vacated the Forest Service's permit for crossing the Appalachian Trail and vacated the Fish and Wildlife Service's biological opinion in August 2018. The Supreme Court reversed the Appalachian Trail ruling in 2020, but multiple other permits remained vacated. On the §106 side, the project faced serious questions about the adequacy of tribal consultation with the Meherrin, Haliwa-Saponi, Coharie, and Lumbee Tribes, and about whether the identification of historic properties in Union Hill — a historic freedman community in Buckingham County, Virginia — was sufficient. The project was cancelled in July 2020 after costs reached $8 billion.

Where the Process Broke Down
I3 — Identify Consulting Parties Agency Action §800.3(f)(2)
Should have happened
FERC makes a reasonable and good faith effort to identify Indian tribes that might attach religious and cultural significance to historic properties in the APE and invites them to be consulting parties.
Actually happened
Tribal consultation with the Meherrin, Haliwa-Saponi, Coharie, and Lumbee Tribes was challenged as inadequate. The route passed through areas historically significant to these tribes, but critics argued the consultation did not meaningfully engage them in shaping the outcome.
Consequence
Tribal consultation adequacy became one of several legal and regulatory vulnerabilities that, combined with permit vacaturs, made the project's risk profile untenable for investors.
ID3 — Identification of Historic Properties SHPO Review §800.4(b)
Should have happened
The agency makes a reasonable and good faith effort to identify historic properties within the APE, including properties of significance to tribes and communities along the route.
Actually happened
The 600-mile route crossed complex terrain spanning three states. The adequacy of identification efforts was questioned, particularly regarding Union Hill — a historic freedman community in Buckingham County, Virginia — where a compressor station was proposed.
Consequence
The Union Hill issue became a focal point for environmental justice concerns, drawing national attention and regulatory scrutiny that further complicated the project's permitting.
AE1 — Assessment of Adverse Effects Adverse Effect §800.5(a)(1)
Should have happened
The agency applies the criteria of adverse effect to determine whether the undertaking would alter the characteristics that qualify historic properties for the National Register — including effects on setting, feeling, and association.
Actually happened
The assessment of effects on Union Hill and on properties of significance to the Meherrin, Haliwa-Saponi, Coharie, and Lumbee Tribes was challenged as insufficient. Critics argued that siting a compressor station in a historic freedman community and routing the pipeline through tribally significant areas required a more thorough adverse effect analysis than was conducted.
Consequence
The §106 concerns reinforced the broader narrative of inadequate environmental justice analysis, contributing to regulatory and political pressure that — alongside permit vacaturs and cost overruns — led to the project's cancellation.
WCA1 — Resolution / Mitigation Resolution §800.6(a)
Should have happened
The agency consults with SHPO/THPO and consulting parties to develop alternatives or modifications that avoid, minimize, or mitigate adverse effects.
Actually happened
The adequacy of mitigation measures for effects on Union Hill and tribal properties was questioned. The resolution process was overtaken by the broader legal and financial collapse of the project — multiple permits were vacated by the Fourth Circuit, and costs spiraled from $5.1 billion to $8 billion.
Consequence
The project was cancelled in July 2020 before the §106 resolution process could fully play out, leaving the question of whether adequate mitigation was achievable permanently unanswered.
Key Takeaway: The Atlantic Coast Pipeline shows how §106 failures compound with other regulatory vulnerabilities to make a project financially unviable. The pipeline was not cancelled because of §106 alone — it was cancelled because inadequate tribal consultation, incomplete identification, and contested adverse effect assessments added legal risk on top of already-vacated permits and ballooning costs. For a 600-mile pipeline crossing three states, the cost of getting §106 wrong was not just delay — it was $8 billion in sunk investment and a project that was never built.
Why pipelines and transmission lines create special problems: These projects stretch across hundreds of miles and cross through multiple states, counties, and tribal lands. That makes the standard case-by-case Section 106 review — where the agency surveys the entire project area before approving anything — nearly impossible to carry out in practice. Three problems come up over and over: (1) Multiple federal agencies may each control only a piece of the project, so no single agency sees the whole picture. (2) The final route may not be known when the agency needs to start the review, so the agency cannot survey the entire corridor up front. (3) When several federal agencies share jurisdiction over different parts of the same pipeline, those agencies must coordinate their Section 106 reviews or risk contradictory findings. The agency-specific procedures described below — including phased identification, programmatic agreements, and custom rules — are all tools the regulations specifically authorize to handle these situations.

Agency-Specific §106 Procedures
⚠ Differs from standard §106 Full route jurisdiction PA common for major projects

Jurisdiction & Undertaking Scope

FERC must authorize the entire route of an interstate natural gas pipeline under Natural Gas Act §7(c). Accordingly, FERC's §106 undertaking is the complete pipeline, not discrete segments — unlike USACE, which has jurisdiction only over water crossings. FERC remains legally responsible for all §106 findings and determinations even when a project sponsor performs the fieldwork.

Project Sponsor Role (§800.2(a)(3))

FERC authorizes project sponsors (the pipeline company) to initiate contact with consulting parties and to prepare all cultural resource reports. Sponsors assist FERC; FERC staff makes every formal determination. Because FERC is a quasi-judicial body bound by the Administrative Procedure Act, off-the-record communications with FERC staff are prohibited — all submissions go through the formal docket.

Two Application Tracks

  • Pre-filing environmental review (required for LNG projects; voluntary for others): Sponsor initiates §106 outreach and fieldwork months before the formal application, giving tribes and SHPOs early notice and time to consult
  • Direct application: Sponsor files the application and the pre-construction §106 process runs concurrent with FERC review

APE Definition for Natural Gas Projects

  • Direct APE: All construction areas — ROW, compressor/meter stations, staging areas, extra-work spaces, access roads, ancillary facilities. The same project may carry different APEs for direct (ground-disturbing) effects vs. indirect (visual, audible, vibratory, atmospheric) effects on aboveground resources
  • Survey corridor: Should be wide enough to accommodate minor route modifications so that route adjustments can still avoid or minimize impacts on resources already identified
  • Indirect APE: Extends beyond the construction footprint for viewshed, noise, and vibration effects on historic structures and landscapes

Boring/Directional Drilling — Avoidance vs. Mitigation (Critical Distinction)

FERC draws a bright-line rule determining whether boring under a historic property is avoidance (no treatment plan required) or mitigation (treatment plan required):

  • Avoidance — no treatment plan when: (1) the pipeline is at least 25 feet from any portion of the historic property, AND (2) no construction, operation, or maintenance will occur within the historic property boundaries. Required docs: bore pit/lay-down area locations, scaled plan/profile drawing, access routes that avoid the property, pre-construction geotechnical studies
  • Mitigation — treatment plan required when either criterion above cannot be met. The treatment plan must include: a contingency plan if boring fails, pre-boring documentation of aboveground features, and long-term maintenance access protocols

Tribal Outreach — Broader Than Standard §106

Sponsors must identify and consult all tribes with current or historical use of or occupancy in the project area — including tribes no longer residing in the region. This is broader than standard §106, which focuses on tribes with demonstrated concern for properties in the APE.

Required Reports (Sequential)

  • Overview Report — background research, existing resource data, proposed APE, identification of consulting parties
  • Survey Report — field methods, resources identified, NRHP eligibility recommendations
  • Evaluation Report — formal NRHP eligibility determinations; if no adverse effect, process ends here
  • Agreement Document — FERC drafts the MOA or PA; project sponsor does not draft it
  • Treatment Plan — mitigation specifics for each adversely affected historic property
  • Management Summary — due within 30 days of completing fieldwork
  • Treatment Report — due within 1 year of completing fieldwork; documents all mitigation completed

Plan for Unanticipated Cultural Resources

Required as part of every application: specifies stop-work procedures if unanticipated resources are discovered during construction. Must address human remains separately under NAGPRA.

Blanket Certificate Program

Minor modifications at existing natural gas facilities (e.g., adding a compressor unit) may qualify for a blanket certificate — a pre-authorized, streamlined FERC process with simplified §106 review rather than the full report sequence above.

⚠ Segmented review (contested) ACHP dispute over undertaking scope NWP 12 for most crossings

Narrow Jurisdiction: Water Crossings Only

The Corps has no authority to approve pipelines along their entire domestic routes. Its jurisdiction is limited to: (1) activities in §404 waters and wetlands; (2) structures/work in §10 navigable waters; and (3) crossings of Corps-managed civil works lands (requiring a §408 permission and easement). Upland pipeline segments on private land are entirely outside Corps jurisdiction and therefore outside Corps §106 review.

Corps' Own §106 Procedures (Appendix C to 33 C.F.R. Part 325)

The Corps follows its own "Procedures for the Protection of Historic Properties" in Appendix C — not the standard 36 C.F.R. Part 800. As of 2016, ACHP has not accepted the Corps' Appendix C as consistent with ACHP regulations (which require ACHP to approve any alternate procedures under §800.14(a)). Some federal district courts have declined to apply Appendix C on grounds of inconsistency with NHPA; others have upheld it. This institutional dispute remains unresolved.

The "Small Federal Handle" Problem

When a major pipeline's only federal touch-points are discrete water crossings, the Corps treats each crossing as a separate undertaking. Critics (including ACHP) argue this fails to assess cumulative effects on historic properties across the entire pipeline corridor. ACHP has formally objected, stating the Corps' approach "dismisses the potential for effects to historic properties that may be located within the broader project area of an undertaking when properly defined." Courts have reached inconsistent conclusions on this dispute.

Nationwide Permit 12 (NWP 12) — Used for Most Pipeline Crossings

  • Pre-authorizes utility-line activities (including oil and gas pipelines) in waters of the U.S. where the activity will not result in loss of more than 0.5 acres of waters
  • General Condition 20 (Historic Properties): No NWP activity may be authorized if it may affect NRHP-eligible properties, until §106 consultation is complete
  • GC 17 (Tribal Rights): No NWP activity may cause more than minimal adverse effects on tribal rights, including treaty rights
  • Individual NWP 12 verifications do NOT require NEPA documentation or a public comment period — public notice happened at the rulemaking stage

Pre-Construction Notification (PCN) and §106 Trigger

For NWP activities, the project applicant — not the Corps — identifies historic properties and submits a PCN. §106 review is triggered only when the applicant raises it in the PCN. Corps then verifies within 45 days (extended if ESA §7 or NHPA §106 consultation is needed). For activities below NWP thresholds that don't require a PCN, applicants self-certify compliance without Corps review.

Individual Permits — Standard §106 Applies

Proposed crossings exceeding NWP 12 thresholds (e.g., >0.5 acre wetland loss) require an individual permit. For individual permits, the Corps district engineer is responsible for identifying historic properties and completing consultations — following standard §800.4–800.6 procedures. Individual permits require public notice, an alternatives analysis, and site-specific NEPA documentation.

Civil Works Easements (Corps Land Crossings)

When a pipeline crosses Corps-managed federal land or a Corps flood easement on private land, the developer needs both a §408 permission (to alter the civil works project) and a real estate easement (Mineral Leasing Act §28). As part of the §408 technical review, the Corps evaluates NEPA and §106 compliance — here the "undertaking" is the crossing of Corps land, with a geographic scope broader than a simple NWP verification.

DAPL as Context

The Dakota Access Pipeline controversy illustrates these tensions: NWP 12 was used for 202 water crossings; no individual §106 consultation was required for the upland route; the easement for the Lake Oahe crossing — 0.55 miles from the Standing Rock Sioux reservation — became the focal point of tribal legal challenges asserting inadequate §106 consultation.

BLM National Programmatic Agreement (nPA)

BLM does not follow the standard §800.3–800.6 process for routine undertakings. Instead, the BLM National PA (executed with ACHP and NCSHPO; current version amended 2024) governs all BLM §106 compliance through state-specific BLM-SHPO protocols. Each state BLM office maintains a protocol with at least 17 required elements that streamline identification, evaluation, and resolution for routine undertakings. Annual reports track implementation.

Project-Specific PA for Complex Linear Projects (§800.14(b))

For large linear projects where effects cannot be fully determined before authorization (e.g., a 211-mile industrial road, a pipeline corridor crossing diverse terrain), BLM executes a project-specific PA that establishes a phased alternative to §§800.3–800.6. This PA is developed through §800.6 consultation and Council participation is mandatory (§800.6(a)(1)(i)(C)) because a PA will be the resolution document.

NEPA–NHPA Coordination Requirement

Per BLM Instruction Memorandum 2012-108: BLM must complete both NHPA §106 and tribal consultation prior to making a final decision. A categorical exclusion under NEPA does not satisfy §106 — both processes run concurrently but separately. BLM uses a side-by-side process chart to track parallel NEPA and §106 milestones.

Permittee Responsibilities

The project developer (permittee) funds and implements all cultural resource work — survey, evaluation, treatment — under BLM direction. No ground disturbance may occur in any project segment until BLM provides written notice that §106 requirements are satisfied for that segment.

GIS Predictive Modeling & Risk-Based Survey

For multi-hundred-mile corridors, 100% pedestrian survey is impractical pre-authorization. BLM's phased PA approach uses:

  • GIS archaeological potential model — categorizes the entire APE as High, Medium, or Low potential based on landforms, known site locations, water features, vegetation, and other variables
  • Differentiated survey coverage: 100% of High potential areas; 50% of Medium; 10% of Low (within the direct APE)
  • Model refined annually as new fieldwork data are collected
  • Indirect APE: Subsurface survey not required unless reasonably foreseeable adverse effects; only surface resources addressed

Alternative Four Step Process — Annual Cycle

The phased PA replaces the single-event §800.4–800.6 review with an annual cycle tied to construction phases and project segments:

  • Annual Work Plan (submitted by March 1 or 60 days before first fieldwork) — Survey strategy, GIS model updates, schedule; approved by BLM and SHPO before implementation
  • Interim Report — Indirect APE (within 30 days post-fieldwork) — Identifies Indirect APE resources; 15-day consulting party review to identify which warrant evaluation
  • Annual Fieldwork Report (within 90 days post-fieldwork) — NRHP eligibility recommendations, effect findings, proposed mitigation; 45-day consulting party review; BLM submits DOEs to SHPO within 15 days
  • Treatment Plans (within 120 days of SHPO acceptance of DOEs) — Property-specific mitigation plans; 30-day review; approved by BLM and SHPO
  • Final Implementation Report (within 180 days of treatment completion) — Comprehensive record; BLM approval = §106 satisfied for that historic property

Multi-Agency Coordination

BLM designates itself as lead federal agency under §800.2(a)(2). Other agencies with jurisdiction over portions of the corridor (NPS for national preserve crossings; USACE for water crossings; State agencies for state land) sign as invited signatories and enforce the PA within their own jurisdiction. Each must provide written notice to BLM when §106 is satisfied within their scope before BLM authorizes construction in that segment.

  • NRHP eligibility re-evaluation every 5 years or following significant design changes
  • Re-survey required if >10 years have passed since original survey and design changes affect surveyed areas
  • Broad-scale cumulative effects addressed within 3 years of completing initial inventory

Understanding Program Alternatives — §800.14
What is a "program alternative"? Sometimes the standard step-by-step Section 106 process does not fit the situation — for example, when an agency handles thousands of similar small projects every year, or when a project is too large and complex to survey completely before the agency can give approval. Section 800.14 of the regulations lets federal agencies and the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation set up alternative approaches that replace some or all of the standard review steps. When a project is already covered by one of these alternatives, the agency follows that alternative instead of the standard process — this is the early exit shown in the flowchart at the §800.3 stage. There are five types, each designed for a different situation.

① Alternate Procedures

§800.14(a)

A federal agency writes its own set of rules for carrying out Section 106, replacing some or all of the standard steps. The agency must make sure these custom rules are consistent with the Council's regulations, and the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation gets 60 days to review the rules before the agency can adopt them. Once the Council approves, the agency follows its own rules instead of the standard process.

  • The agency must consult with the Council, SHPOs/THPOs, tribes, and the public while developing the procedures
  • The agency publishes the final procedures in the Federal Register
  • These custom rules cannot override tribal historic preservation regulations on tribal lands

② Programmatic Agreements (PA)

§800.14(b) · §800.16(t)

A Programmatic Agreement is a negotiated deal between the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation and a federal agency that covers an entire program or a group of complex projects at once. Unlike a Memorandum of Agreement — which covers one specific project — a PA covers every project in the program until the agreement expires or one of the parties ends it.

When an agency can use a PA:

  • The projects' effects on historic properties are similar and repeat across many locations or states
  • The agency cannot fully identify the effects before giving approval — for example, the final route is not yet known
  • Non-federal parties (like a pipeline company) handle major decision-making on the agency's behalf
  • The agency routinely manages federal land or installations and needs a streamlined process
  • Other circumstances make the standard step-by-step process impractical

Who signs: The Council, the agency, and the appropriate SHPO or THPO all sign. For nationwide programs, the president of the National Conference of State Historic Preservation Officers signs in place of individual SHPOs. On tribal lands, the THPO or a tribal representative must sign.

Prototype PA shortcut (§800.14(b)(4)): If the Council has already designated a prototype PA for a certain type of program, the agency can use it with just the SHPO or THPO — the Council does not need to participate or sign.

How the agency develops a PA: For complex or multi-project situations, the agency develops the PA through the §800.6 consultation process. The Council's participation is mandatory — the agency must invite the Council to the table whenever a PA will be the resolution document.

PA vs. MOA:

  • MOA — covers one specific project where the agency can identify the effects up front; resolves adverse effects on known historic properties
  • PA — covers a program or multiple complex projects where the agency cannot identify all effects in advance; compliance with the PA satisfies Section 106 for every project the agreement covers

③ Exempted Categories

§800.14(c)

The Advisory Council on Historic Preservation or a federal agency can propose to exempt an entire category of projects from the Section 106 review. To get the exemption approved, the following must be true:

  • The projects would otherwise count as "undertakings" requiring Section 106 review
  • The projects' potential effects on historic properties are predictable and likely to be minimal or not harmful
  • Exempting the projects is consistent with the goals of the National Historic Preservation Act

The Council has 30 days to approve or reject the exemption. Once the Council approves, projects in that category skip the Section 106 review entirely — unless the agency or the Council decides a specific project needs individual review after all.

④ Standard Treatments

§800.14(d)

The Advisory Council on Historic Preservation can create a pre-approved playbook for handling a common type of situation — for example, a standard protocol for what to do when workers discover archaeological sites during construction. When a federal agency faces that type of situation, the agency can use the Council's standard treatment as the starting point for a Memorandum of Agreement instead of negotiating every term from scratch.

  • The Council creates standard treatments on its own initiative or when another party requests one
  • The Council publishes each standard treatment in the Federal Register
  • The Council can end a standard treatment by giving 30 days' notice

⑤ Program Comments

§800.14(e)

A federal agency can ask the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation to issue one set of comments covering an entire category of projects, so that the agency does not have to run a separate Section 106 review for each individual project. The Council has 45 days to respond.

  • The agency describes the category of projects, the likely effects on historic properties, and what steps the agency will take to address those effects
  • If the Council issues comments: the agency takes the Council's comments into account for every project in that category; the Council publishes the comments in the Federal Register
  • If the Council declines to comment: the agency must go back to running individual Section 106 reviews for each project
  • The Council can withdraw its program comment if the agency is not properly accounting for effects on historic properties

Special Situations: Emergency & Post-Review Discoveries

§800.12 · §800.13

Emergency situations (§800.12): When a federal agency must act quickly to respond to a declared disaster or emergency, the agency can satisfy Section 106 in two ways: (1) following an existing Programmatic Agreement that already includes emergency provisions, or (2) notifying the Council, the SHPO or THPO, and affected tribes and giving those parties 7 days to comment — or less if the emergency demands faster action. This shortcut applies only to projects the agency carries out within 30 days of the disaster or emergency declaration.

Post-review discoveries (§800.13): Sometimes workers discover historic properties after the Section 106 review is already finished. What happens next depends on whether construction has started:

  • Best practice: The agency should include discovery procedures in the MOA or PA before any work begins, so that everyone knows what to do if something turns up
  • If construction has not started: the agency consults with the SHPO or THPO and other parties under §800.6 to figure out how to handle the discovery
  • If construction has started: the agency must notify the SHPO or THPO, affected tribes, and the Council within 48 hours; all parties must respond within 48 hours; the agency takes those recommendations into account before resuming work
  • On tribal lands: the agency must also follow applicable tribal regulations and get the tribe's concurrence

Key Definitions — §800.16
Undertaking §800.16(y) Any project, activity, or program that a federal agency funds (even partly), carries out, or approves. This includes projects that a private company runs if the company needs a federal permit, license, or financial assistance. If the federal government has any hand in a project, that project is an "undertaking" and Section 106 applies.
Historic Property §800.16(l) Any district, site, building, structure, or object — prehistoric or historic — that is listed on the National Register of Historic Places or that qualifies for listing. A property does not have to be officially listed to count; if it meets the National Register criteria, the agency must treat it as a historic property. This includes places of traditional religious and cultural importance to tribes or Native Hawaiian organizations, as long as those places meet the National Register criteria.
Effect §800.16(i) Any change to a historic property that alters the qualities making that property worthy of the National Register. An "effect" can be physical (like demolition or construction damage), visual (like a new building blocking a historic viewshed), or environmental (like noise or pollution that changes the property's character).
Area of Potential Effects (APE) §800.16(d) The boundary the agency draws around every area where a project could change historic properties — directly or indirectly. The agency must consider all types of effects: physical ground disturbance, visual changes, noise, and atmospheric impacts. A single project can have different APEs for different types of effects — for example, a wider boundary for visual impacts than for physical construction.
Consultation §800.16(f) The back-and-forth process where the agency asks for, listens to, and seriously considers the views of other parties involved in the Section 106 review — and, where possible, works toward agreement. Consultation is not just sending a letter and waiting; the agency must genuinely engage. When tribes are involved, the agency must conduct government-to-government consultation, recognizing tribes as sovereign nations.
Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) §800.16(o) A legally binding agreement that spells out exactly what the agency will do to avoid, reduce, or offset adverse effects on historic properties from a specific project. The agency, the SHPO or THPO, and (if participating) the Council all sign the MOA. Once signed, the MOA governs the project and all of the project's parts, and the agency must file a copy with the Council.
Programmatic Agreement (PA) §800.16(t) A legally binding agreement that covers an entire agency program, a complex project, or a group of related projects — unlike an MOA, which covers just one project. Compliance with the PA satisfies Section 106 for every individual project the agreement covers, until the PA reaches its expiration date or one of the signatories terminates the agreement.
Foreclosure §800.16(j) When an agency takes an action — like starting construction or committing funds — that makes it impossible for the Council's comments to matter anymore, because the agency can no longer meaningfully change course. The law prohibits foreclosure. The agency must complete the Section 106 review before spending federal money or issuing a license, not after.
Comment §800.16(e) The formal, written findings and recommendations that the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation sends to the head of a federal agency under Section 106. Formal comments carry more weight than the Council's advisory opinions earlier in the process — the head of the agency must personally consider formal comments before making a final decision.
SHPO §800.16(v) State Historic Preservation Officer — the official each state appoints to run the state's historic preservation program. The SHPO is the agency's primary partner in the Section 106 review for most projects. The SHPO reviews the agency's findings, concurs or objects, and signs any Memorandum of Agreement.
THPO §800.16(w) Tribal Historic Preservation Officer — a tribal official who has formally taken over the SHPO's Section 106 responsibilities for projects on that tribe's lands. When a THPO is in place, the agency works with the THPO instead of (or alongside) the SHPO for projects on or affecting tribal lands.
National Historic Landmark (NHL) §800.16(p) A historic property that the Secretary of the Interior has designated as one of the nation's most significant historic places. When a project would adversely affect a National Historic Landmark, the stakes go up: the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation must participate in the consultation, and the agency must notify the Secretary of the Interior.

About This Project

Project Overview

This interactive map walks through the Section 106 review process under the National Historic Preservation Act, step by step. The map brings together three layers: (1) the standard regulatory process that every federal agency must follow under 36 C.F.R. Part 800, (2) the specific ways FERC, the Army Corps of Engineers, and BLM modify that process for pipelines and other linear energy projects, and (3) data showing which steps federal courts most often scrutinize when someone sues over a Section 106 decision.

Methodology

The project team built this map by reading the full text of 36 C.F.R. Part 800 and its official guidance documents. The team drew FERC, Army Corps, and BLM procedures from each agency's published guidance, programmatic agreements, and regulatory filings.

The litigation data comes from a keyword analysis of 557 federal court decisions involving energy infrastructure projects (pipelines, transmission lines, power plants, wind and solar facilities, and mining) where Section 106 was a central issue. The decisions come from district courts, circuit courts, and the Supreme Court, all sourced from Lexis Advance.
